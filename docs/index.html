Which Wrocław dwarf is this?

Wrocław is scattered with several hundred small bronze dwarf statues, individually sculpted but sharing a vocabulary — the same scale, the same material, the same crouching poses and props. Upload a photograph and this page will tell you which one it is, running the model in your own browser.

    What the experiments found

    95.9%
    top-1, full pool

    Frozen embeddings are enough. DINOv2 identifies the right dwarf first time in 95.9% of queries and within five candidates 99.3% of the time — with no training of any kind. CLIP, which is what runs above, reaches 92.5%.

    −0.96
    points / doubling

    The decay is shallow, and the backbones diverge. Each doubling of the candidate pool costs DINOv2 about one accuracy point and CLIP 1.8. They are identical at two candidates and six points apart at 23.

    −1.6
    points from location

    Narrowing by location helps less than the simulation says. Real proximity-based pools are harder than random pools of the same size, because the statues that are hardest to tell apart were installed together.

    +0.7
    points from training

    A trained classifier does not help. A linear probe beats nearest-neighbour retrieval by one query in 146; class prototypes lose two to three points. At this scale, retrieval is already the right tool.

    6 / 146
    errors, dinov2

    The mistakes are explainable. Both backbones concentrate their errors on the same small cluster of water-themed dwarves — which the projection, the confusion analysis and the map all independently pick out.

    Geographic pools are harder than random pools of the same size. CLIP shows it more strongly: −2.5 points at a pool of five, −2.1 at eight, −1.9 at ten. Small in absolute terms, but it runs the wrong way at almost every size — and it has a clear cause.

    The statues you can't separate are standing together

    Seven of the 23 dwarves stand at one spot — a single themed installation that includes Puszczający Stateczki, Zbierający Wodę and Karmiący Ptaki.

    Those are the same statues the confusion analysis flags, and the same tight neighbourhood the embedding projection shows. Three of DINOv2's five confused pairs are zero metres apart.

    The dwarves that are hardest to tell apart were installed as a group — so they share a sculptor, a pose vocabulary and a location. Location narrowing cannot separate them; it guarantees they land in the same pool. Random subsampling scatters them across different pools, and so overstates what narrowing buys.

    For a location-aware tool: a 400 m radius around a visitor covers about ten candidates, and identification within that pool runs at 96.6% top-1 for DINOv2 — useful, but no better than ten dwarves picked at random, for exactly the reason that makes the problem interesting.

    Does training beat retrieval?

    All three methods scored on the same leave-one-out folds, with one classifier fitted per fold so no query ever sits in its own training data.

    MethodDINOv2CLIPvs. retrieval
    Cosine retrieval95.9%92.5%—
    Linear probe96.6%93.2%+0.7
    Class prototypes93.2%90.4%−2.7 / −2.1

    The probe's win is a single extra correct query, well inside the confidence intervals. Prototypes actively hurt: averaging a class into one vector throws away the pose and viewpoint variation that makes a particular photograph matchable.

    One detail worth keeping, because it nearly became a wrong conclusion. At scikit-learn's default C=1.0 the probe scored 66% — which reads as a decisive result and is really just underfitting, since L2-normalised embeddings put per-dimension magnitudes near 1/√d and that penalty crushes the weights. At C=100 it scores 96.6%. A badly regularised baseline is worse than no baseline.

    Where the errors are

    Outright errors are rare, so every query contributes its strongest wrong candidate, not just the failures. That is where the signal is on a dataset this size.

    What this does not show

    23 classes is a small pool. The full pool already sits near the accuracy ceiling, so the curve is shallow and extrapolating past it is unreliable. Extrapolated to the city's ~1,400 dwarves the fit gives 88.5%, but that is an optimistic bound, not a prediction.
    The demo above is not the pipeline that produced these numbers. It runs CLIP, which is small enough for a browser, where the results report DINOv2 as three points better. Its own leave-one-out score, measured on exactly the vectors it ships, is 91.8% top-1 against 92.5% for the research CLIP pipeline — a difference of one query in 146, caused by the browser resizing images slightly differently. Append ?selftest=1 to this page's URL to make it check that for itself.
    The reference photos are not phone photos. Wikimedia Commons uploads mean good light and considered framing, so a casual snapshot is a harder query than anything measured here.
    There is no “I don't know”. A statue outside the 23 — or a photograph of something else entirely — still returns its nearest neighbours. There is no open-set rejection.

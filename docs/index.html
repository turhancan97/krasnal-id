Abstract

Instance retrieval benchmarks are dominated by landmarks, which are large and distinctive by design. We study the opposite case: objects made to look alike. We introduce Doppeldwarf-306: 1,691 openly licensed photographs of 306 bronze dwarf statues in one European city, many made in themed series that share one style, with the photographer of each image recorded. With no training, nearest-neighbour search over frozen features names the right statue first for up to 94.7% of queries. Across thirteen backbones from seven pretraining paradigms, models trained for invariant embeddings lead, while masked-prediction models (MAE, BEiT, I-JEPA) reach 55.6% at best under a fixed pooling rule. For DINOv2, accuracy falls slowly as more statues are added, by about 0.8 points each time the number of candidates doubles. A natural idea for a phone guide is to compare a photograph only with statues near the user. Surprisingly, in simulation this helps less than comparing it with the same number of statues chosen at random, because the few lookalikes that stand close together stay in a neighbourhood pool; a permutation test shows the penalty depends on the real layout (p = 0.005). Used as a weight instead of a filter, location raises accuracy from 93.4% to 98.7% for a simulated user 25 m from the statue. When the photographer is new, learned local-feature matching adds 2.5 to 3.6 points, and still helps DINOv3, while SIFT adds nothing measurable. Finally, comparing the two best candidates halves how often an unknown statue is wrongly accepted, for DINOv2 and DINOv3.

Key results

Frozen features, the same statues, and a sequence of tests of what helps.

Accuracy falls slowly as candidates are added

DINOv2 loses 0.79 accuracy points per doubling of the candidate pool, reaching 93.1% top-1 across all 306 statues. CLIP loses 2.06 points per doubling, and its decline steepens as the pool grows.

Location helps more as a weight than as a filter

Nearby candidate pools are harder than random pools of the same size: a few close-standing lookalikes stay in the neighbourhood pool. Using distance as a soft weight instead raises DINOv2 from 93.4% to 98.7% for a simulated user 25 m from the statue. This experiment covers the 294 of 306 statues with coordinates, most derived from camera positions.

Pretraining decides most of the outcome

Across thirteen frozen backbones in seven pretraining paradigms, models trained for invariant embeddings lead, with C-RADIOv3 reaching 94.7% top-1. Masked-prediction models MAE, BEiT and I-JEPA fail as frozen retrieval features under the fixed pooling rule, reaching only 47–56%.

Learned verification helps where SIFT does not

With the query’s photographer withheld, DISK+LightGlue and LoFTR lift DINOv2 top-1 from 81.8% to 84.4% and 85.5%, with weights chosen on held-out statues. SIFT adds nothing measurable; learned verification still adds 2.0 points on DINOv3.

Rejection needs a margin

A high similarity alone is weak evidence that a statue is known when the pool contains many lookalikes. At 90% known-statue acceptance, comparing the best two statues lowers false acceptance from 38.3% to 19.6% for DINOv2 and from 21.6% to 7.7% for DINOv3.

These are research-pipeline measurements. The identifier reports its own measurements on the browser vectors it ships. Street photographs remain an unmeasured domain gap.

The dataset

Every image carries its source, photographer, licence and licence URL. Coverage follows usable photographs rather than geography; each statue has at least three references. Coordinates are recorded as Wikidata positions or positions derived from Commons camera metadata.

The photographs retain their individual terms: CC BY-SA (1,577), CC BY (55), public domain (52) and CC0 (7). Per-file attribution and modification records accompany the dataset.

The statues are contemporary sculptures by living artists, protected independently of the photographs. Wikimedia Commons hosts these photographs under Poland’s freedom-of-panorama provision for works permanently displayed in public places. The United States, where the dataset platforms are hosted, has no equivalent exemption for sculpture. The photographers’ Creative Commons licences cover the photographs and cannot grant rights in the sculptures depicted; reuse requiring the sculptor’s permission still requires it.

Removal requests from sculptors, photographers, rights-holders, their representatives or people depicted are honoured without requiring a legal argument. Open an issue; the photograph is excluded through data/image-review.json, the affected artifacts are rebuilt, and the dataset is updated.
